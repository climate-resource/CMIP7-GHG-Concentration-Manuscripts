To derive our dataset, we largely follow the methods described in \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}.
However, we have made changes in a number of places
so we describe the method in full,
while also highlighting places of clear divergence.

\subsection{General approach}

In line with \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017},
the general approach is to first create a dataset with monthly temporal resolution
and 15\textdegree~latitudinal spatial resolution
(but no longitudinal resolution).
We refer to this as our `native resolution'.
From our native resolution, we can then create higher and lower resolution data products (see [TODO: forward refernence]).

To create our native resolution dataset, we sum three components:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Global-, annual-mean concentrations
  \item Seasonal variations
  \item Latitudinal gradient
\end{enumerate}

In order to take this sum,
all three components must already have the same time dimension
(monthly timesteps in our case).
This introduces the first subtlety: while our approach is based on a timeseries of global-, annual-mean concentrations,
this timeseries has to be interpolated to monthly timesteps
in order to avoid the change from year to year resulting in step changes between December of one year and January of the next.
The same interpolation requirement applies to the latitudinal gradient
(which is initially calculated on a yearly timestep, see [TODO forward ref]).

In addition to the time dimension consistency requirement, seasonality can vary by latitude.
As a result, the latitudinal variation of seasonality must be calculated
(i.e. seasonality must have a "latitude" dimension)
before taking the sum.

Thus, in practice, we calculate each dataset according to equation \ref{eq:native-resolution-sum}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:native-resolution-sum}
    gn(y, m, l) = gm(y, m) + lg(y, m, l) + s(y, m, l)
\end{equation}
where $gn$ is the native resolution dataset value
(`gn' is the `grid label' used for such output on ESGF, so we re-use it here for convenience and consistency with the published output, see [TODO forward ref]),
$y$ is the year in which the value applies,
$m$ is the month in which the value applies,
$l$ is the latitude at which the value applies,
$gm$ is the global-, annual-mean derived value (and is broadcast across all latitudes),
$lg$ is the latitudinal gradient contribution
and $s$ is the seasonality contribution.

The key to this decomposition is that the latitudinal gradient term, $lg$,
has a spatial (area-weighted) mean of zero
and that the seasonality term, $s$,
has an annual-mean of zero
so that they can be added to the global- annual-mean term, $gm$,
without affecting the corresponding global- annual-mean values.

The key choices are about how we arrive at $gm$, $lg$ and $s$ for each greenhouse gas species in our dataset.
We describe the different methods in the following sections.

\subsection{Calculating the general components}
\label{ssec:methods-general-components}

Before moving to how the general components are calculated for individual species,
we first introduce a `baseline' case.
Only N_2O follows this `baseline' case exactly,
but this idea helps illustrates the components which are specific to each species more easily.
The details of this baseline case are deliberately not described here
because each species' processing is described in detail in the sections below.
This overview is simply provided to help the reader get a sense of the overall flow.
% Author note: we may have to bring N_2O first given it is the base case.
% I'm not going to do that yet, but probably will in a next iteration.

The baseline case comporises the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Collect surface observations from available observing networks. These observations provide the surface concentration of the species at different locations and times around the world.
    \item Bin these raw observations spatially, into a coarse 15\textdegree~latitudinal and 60\textdegree~longitudinal grid, and temporally, into monthly bins.
    \item Interpolate the binned observations spatially. Extract the longest time period over which there are no data gaps after spatial interpolation.
    \item Decompose the interpolated observational network based into a global-, annual-mean; relative seasonality (i.e. seasonality, divided by the global-, annual-mean) and latitudinal gradient components
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The latitudinal gradient components are empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs, a function of latitude only) and principal components (PCs, a function of year only) derived using principal component analysis (PCA). The number of EOFs and PCs retained is either one or two, depending on the gas.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item Extend the global-, annual-mean and latitudinal gradient PCs back to year 1
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The global-, annual-mean is extended using ice cores where available, otherwise based on simple assumptions about pre-industrial values and when those pre-industrial values should be reached
        \item The latitudinal gradient PCs are extended using emissions (based on regressions between the emissions and the PCs) or simple linear or constant extrapolation
    \end{enumerate}
    \item Calculate the inputs for Equation \ref{eq:native-resolution-sum}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The global-, annual-mean is converted into a global-, monthly-mean using mean-preserving interpolation
        \item The relative seasonality is converted into an absolute seasonality over all time by multiplying it by the extended global-, annual-mean
        \item The latitudinal gradient is recovered by taking the product of the EOFs and the extended PCs
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Calculating the general components: CO_2}
\label{ssec:methods-co2}

Summary of differences from the baseline case
(Section \ref{ssec:methods-general-components}):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item an absolute seasonality is used, rather than a relative seasonality. The base seasonality and PCA is used to derive a seseasonality change EOF and an accompanying PC
    \item the global-, annual-mean is extended back in time using ice cores and the [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record], rather than ice cores alone.
    \item the seasonality change PC is extended back to year 1850 using a composite of global-mean surface air temperature and global-, annual-mean CO_2 concentrations (based on a regression between the PC and this composite) and is held constant before this time.
    \item the seasonality component of Equation \ref{eq:native-resolution-sum} is calculated by multiplying the seasonality change EOF with the extended PC, then adding this seasonality change onto the base seasonality to get the seasonality over time.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Observational network}
\label{sssec:methods-co2-observational-network}

CO_2 has the longest observational record of all the greenhouse gases included in our dataset.
It is also the largest driver of climate change to date [TODO citation], hence has the most available observations.
Finally, atmospheric CO_2 concentrations are driven by the carbon cycle,
which led to the introduction of extra complexity in the calculation of the input components,
with the aim of better representing the variations seen in the observational record.

We begin by collecting observations of surface CO_2 concentrations from the NOAA observational networks
[TODO check whether in-situ and flask or just one, and what this means in terms of assuming scales are the same etc.].
We bin these observations into 15\textdegree~latitudinal, 60\textdegree~longitudinal and monthly temporal bins.
Within each bin, we firstly take the mean of all measurements that come from the same network
(where, for NOAA, we treat the in-situ and surface flask measurements as separate networks),
station and measurement method.
[TODO figure out what different measurement methods are]
As a result, each network, station and measurement method gets an equal weight in each bin,
irrespective of how many measurements that network-station-measurement made in the bin.
Then, we take the average over network, station and measurement method
to get a single value in each bin.

\subsubsection{Spatial interpolation}
\label{sssec:methods-co2-spatial-interpolation}

The surface observational network does not cover the entire globe [TODO: figure],
hence the next step is to interpolate the available measurements to get complete spatial coverage.
We specify that at least five data points are required for us to do spatial interpolation
and we must have data points in both the most northern and most southern latitudinal box.
This is clearly a relatively low data requirement,
but higher thresholds result in a significant amount of the observational record being discarded.
More robust interpolation methods are an area for future work [TODO: forward ref].
We then take the longest (in time) dataset that is continuous.
In this dataset, that meant that we used data from 1981 to 2023 (both inclusive).

We use 2D interpolation
to go from the coverage provided by the underlying observational networks
to complete global coverage.
The 2D interpolation is linear barycentric interpolation,
as implemented in scipy's griddata function [TODO ref scipy].
Before interpolating, we put the data on a grid that is `wrapped around' to avoid edge effects.
In practice, this means that we duplicate the data three times
and place these three copies next to each other in the longitudinal direction.
As a result, each point in the central `copy' `sees' its closest neighbours, irrespective of its longitude.
After interpolating, we throw away the outer `copies'
and are left with an `edge effect free' interpolated dataset over the domain of interest.

\subsubsection{Principal component analysis derived components}
\label{sssec:methods-co2-pca-derived-components}

We then take our spatially complete dataset and perform principal component analysis.

% \subsubsubsubsection{zonal mean}

The first step is to calculate the longitudinal (zonal) mean.
This is done using a basic mean as each grid cell has the same area for a given latitude.

% \subsubsubsubsection{global-, annual-mean}

The second step is to calculate the global-, annual-mean.
We do this using an area-weighted mean
and then an annual-mean.
We weight each month equally.
Weighting the months by their length would make a minor difference,
but requires assuming that the processed data is truly representative of the entire month.
We don't make that assumption because we often have point measurements
i.e. measurements taken at a particular point in time
and grouping them in months is a necessary processing step
that should not be taken to mean
that the monthly values robustly represent the average over the entire month, given the month's length.

% \subsubsubsubsection{latitudinal gradient}

The third step is to calculate our latitudinal gradient.
We do this by first calculating the residuals at each latitude,
i.e. the difference between the annual-mean of the longitudinal mean and the global-, annual-mean.
On these residuals, we then perform principal component analysis.
We do this by using numpy's [TODO ref] singular value decomposition function (numpy.linalg.svd [TODO figure out monospace font]).
We translate the singular value decomposition output into two parts.
The first is our empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs) ([TODO check name in M17] referred to as principal components in M17).
These quantify the spatial, in this case latitudinal, variation in the dataset (and are a function of latitude only).
The second is our principal components (PCs) ([TODO check name in M17] referred to as principal component scores in M17).
These quantify the temporal variation in the dataset (and are a function of year only).
If all EOFs are retained, then the product of the EOFs and the PCs
reproduces the residuals we started with exactly.
However, following \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}
we only retain the first two EOFs as these are sufficient
to reproduce the residuals to within 1~ppm CO_2 (and are usually much closer than this).
[TODO figure and calculation of variance explained to make this easier to justify or not]

% \subsubsubsubsection{seasonality}

The fourth step is to calculate our seasonality.
We do this by first calculating the residuals at each time and latitude.
The first component in this calculation is our longitudinal mean (dimensions (year, month, lat)).
The second component is the annual-mean at each latitude,
interpolated to monthly timesteps (as a result, this data also has dimensions (year, month, lat)).
We then calculate the residuals by subtracting the monthly-interpolated annual-mean at each latitude
from the longitudinal mean.
The interpolation to monthly timesteps is necessary
so that the residuals do not include step changes at each year boundary
(i.e. transition from December of year $Y$ to January of year $Y + 1$).
The seasonality is the average of these residuals over all available years
and thus has dimensions (month, lat).

On top of this seasonality, we also calculate EOFs and PCs for the change in seasonality over time.
These are calculated by taking the seasonality and residuals from the previous step.
We then calculate the difference between these two, which we call `seasonality anomalies'.
We then perform singular value decomposition on these seasonality anomalies
(this is done by stacking the data so it has one dimension which is year
and the latitude and month dimensions are stacked into a single dimension
so that the singular value decomposition has a 2D dataset to work with).
As for the latitudinal gradient, we convert the singular value decomposition results
into EOFs and PCs.
However, in the case of seasonality, while the PCs are still only a function of year,
the EOFs have dimensions (latitude, month).
For the seasonality change, again following \cite{meinshausen_historical_2017},
we retain only the first EOF (although this results in larger differences, up to 2~ppm,
between the seasonality anomalies and the EOF-based reproduction
than was the case for the EOF-based latitudinal gradient).
[TODO: figure here or in results, or make this section data and results, maybe]

At this point we have (or, more precisely, can construct),
all the required input components over the time period covered by the observational network.
The last step is to extend these intermediate data products
so that we can produce the required input components
over the entire time period included in the output dataset.

\subsubsection{Extending components over time}
\label{sssec:methods-co2-extending-components-in-time}

% \subsubsubsubsection{Latitudinal gradient}
To extend the latitudinal gradient in time, we need to extend the PCs in time (the EOFs have no time dimension).

Following \cite{meinshausen_historical_2017},
the first PC is extended by performing a linear regression against CO_2 emissions of a geological origin
(as opposed to CO_2 emissions that originate in the active biosphere)
from the [TODO cite PRIMAP version] dataset.
Using this regression, we then extend emissions back to the 1750,
the first year reported in the [TODO cite PRIMAP] dataset.
Before 1750, we keep the first PC value constant.

The second PC is much simpler.
It is simply linearly interpolated between its value at the start of the observational period,
in our case 1981, and zero in 1850.
It is kept constant at zero before 1850.

We use all data from [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] which is not already covered in the observational record.

To avoid introducing an artificial step in the output,

\subsubsection{Finalising the general components}
\label{sssec:methods-co2-finalising-general-components}

The last step is to construct the inputs needed for Equation \ref{eq:native-resolution-sum}
from the temporally extended components.

% \subsubsubsubsection{Interpolating global- annual-mean to monthly timesteps}

We start with the global-, annual-mean term, $gm(y, m)$.
This must be interpolated onto monthly timesteps.
We do this using [TODO find name], which allows us to perform a mean-preserving interpolation
that ensures that the interpolated timeseries stays consistent with the global- annual-mean values.
[TODO note somewhere that this is one of the most computationally expensive steps in the whole process]

% \subsubsubsubsection{Constructing seasonality over time}

Next we calculate the seasonality term, $s(y, m, l)$.
This requires first calculating the seasonality change over time
by taking the product of the temporally extended PC and the EOF.
This seasonality change is then added onto our `base seasonality' to give the full seasonality term.

% \subsubsubsubsection{Constructing latitudinal gradient over time (including interpolating PCs to monthly timesteps)}

Finally we calculate the latitudinal gradient term, $lg(y, m, l)$.
Like for the global-, annual-mean,
to avoid jumps at year boundaries, we must first interpolate the PCs to monthly timesteps
(again using mean-preserving interpolation).
Then the full latitudinal gradient is calculated
by taking the product of the temporally extended, monthly interpolated PCs and the EOFs.

We start with the ice core data from [TODO cite Law Dome].
We smooth this following the method used in \cite{meinshausen_historical_2017}.
To each data point in the [TODO cite Law Dome] dataset,
we add random noise.
The noise is normally distributed.
In the value axis, it has a standard deviation of 3~ppb.
In the time axis, the standard deviation increases as the age of the observation increases
according to the equation $\sigma = 0.025 \times (2024 - y)$, where $y$ is the year to which the observation applies.
For each year between the start and end of the [TODO cite Law Dome] dataset, we pick at least four points either side of that year
and a maximum of ten points either side of that year.
If there are four or more points within 100 years of the year of interest
(on a given side, i.e. before or after the year of interest),
the points that are closest to the year for which we are trying to generate a value are used (on that side).
If there are fewer than four points within 100 years of the window of interest
(on a given side, i.e. before or after the year of interest),
then the nearest four points on that side are used, irrespective of how far away they are from the year of interest.
Based on the selected points, a quantile regression with a third order polynomial is performed.
The inferred value for the year of interest is then taken from this regression.
This process is repeated over all years, and the entire process is then repeated 250 times.
The smoothed dataset is then the median over these 250 repeats.

\subsection{Derived output grids}

For each gas, we apply Equation \ref{eq:native-resolution-sum}
to create a gridded dataset on our 15\textdegree~latitudinal (spatial), monthly (temporal) `native' grid.
From this native grid output, we then derive four other output products:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item hemispheric (spatial) monthly (temporal) grid
    \item global-mean (spatial) monthly (temporal) grid
    \item hemispheric (spatial) yearly (temporal) grid
    \item global-mean (spatial) yearly (temporal) grid
\end{enumerate}

These outputs are trivially derived by taking means in the relevant axes
(where the spatial means are area-weighted).
The outputs are then formatted before being published on the ESGF for distribution to modelling teams.

We also have the ability to produce higher-resolution spatial grids
by applying mean-preserving interpolation to the latitudinal axis of the native grid output.
Arbitrary latitudinal resolution can be produced by applying this technique.

%   if anyone ever needs these outputs.
As a result of the processing time, data storage requirements and lack of use in CMIP6,
we have not produced the 0.5\textdegree~latitudinal grid product as part of our `standard' offering this time.

\subsection{Equivalent species}

Given that the majority, it not all, ESMs will not want the computational expense of including 43 greenhouse gas tracers,
we also produce the equivalent species provided in \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}.
These equivalent species allow modelling teams to pick one of three options when running:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Use all GHGs individually: modelling teams can pick which of the 43 provided GHGs to run with. Note that running with any of the 43 GHGs missing means that some GHG radiative effects will be missing from the simulation
    \item Use CO_2, CH_4, N_2O, CFC-12 and CFC-11 equivalent: CFC-11 captures the equivalent effect of all GHGs other than CO_2, CH_4, N_2O and CFC-12.
    \item Use CO_2, CH_4, N_2O, CFC-12 equivalent and HFC-134a equivalent: CFC-12 captures the equivalent effect of all ozone-depleting substances (namely CFC-11, CFC-12, CFC-113, CFC-114, CFC-115, CCl_4, CH_2Cl_2, CH_3Br, CH_3CCl_3, CH_3Cl, CHCl_3, Halon-1211, Halon-1301, Halon-2402, HCFC-141b, HCFC-142b, HCFC-22) while all other fluorinated gases are included in HFC-134a (namely CF_4, C_2F_6, C_3F_8, cC_4F_8, C_4F_10, C_5F_12, C_6F_14, C_7F_16, C_8F_18, HFC-23, HFC-32, HFC-125, HFC-134a, HFC-143a, HFC-152a, HFC-227ea, HFC-236fa, HFC-245fa, HFC-365mfc, HFC-4310mee, NF_3, SF_6, SO_2F_2)
\end{enumerate}

The equivalence is calculated following the `full equivalence' definition given in \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}.
[TODO in the citation, add see their Equation 7].
We use the radiative efficiencies from the last IPCC Assessment Report,
specifically the radiative efficiencies given in Table 7.SM.6 of the Working Group 1 Chapter 7 Supplementary Material [TODO citation].

These equivalent species are derived based on output that is already on the desired output grid,
rather than on the native grid (with further processing applied thereafter).
This avoids any consistency issues due to non-linearity in the processing operations,
such as from mean-preserving interpolation.

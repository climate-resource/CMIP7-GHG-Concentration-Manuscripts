\subsection{General approach}
\label{ssec:methods-general-approach}

In line with \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017},
we provide datasets of atmospheric concentrations for a wide selection of greenhouse gases.
No dependency on longitude is provided.
For a brief discussion on how add a vertical dimension to these datasets, see section \ref{ssec:methods-vertical}.

For all greenhouse gases, the first dataset
that we generate has monthly temporal resolution and 15\textdegree~latitudinal spatial resolution.
We refer to this grid as our `native resolution', and therefore to these datasets
as our `native resolution datasets'.
From the native resolution datasets, we create higher and lower resolution data products
(see section \ref{ssec:methods-derived-grids}).

Although there are differences in the methods and limitations for each gas, the general approach can
be broken down as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item {
        \textbf{Step 1: Collect observations}

        The first step is collecting surface observations from available observing networks.
        These networks provide surface concentrations at different locations and times around the world.

        Where we have a choice, we use data that is already aggregated to a monthly temporal resolution, rather than raw data.
        We make this choice because we believe that the raw data providers are best placed to produce monthly aggregations from the raw data,
        including making subjective choices such as which measurements are appropriate to include in such an aggregation,
        how to weight measurements and how to treat raw measurements taken at different points within each month.
        This is different to \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}, which used the raw data directly and performed their own aggregation step.
    }

     \item {
         \textbf{Step 2: Bin and interpolate observations}

         The raw observations are binned spatially, into a coarse 15\textdegree~latitudinal
         and 60\textdegree~longitudinal grid, and temporally, into monthly bins.
         Then, since the surface observational network does not cover the entire globe,
         we interpolate the binned observations spatially to get complete global coverage.

         We specify that at least five data points are required for us to do spatial interpolation
         and we must have data points in both the most northern and most southern latitudinal box.
         This is clearly a relatively low data requirement,
         but higher thresholds result in a significant amount of the observational record being discarded.
         More robust interpolation methods are an area for future work [TODO: forward ref].

         We use 2D interpolation, specifically linear barycentric interpolation,
         as implemented in scipy's griddata function [TODO ref scipy].
         Before interpolating, we put the data on a grid that is `wrapped around' to avoid edge effects.
         In practice, this means that we duplicate the data three times
         and place these three copies next to each other in the longitudinal direction.
         As a result, each point in the central `copy' `sees' its closest neighbours, irrespective of its longitude.
         After interpolating, we throw away the outer `copies'
         and are left with an `edge effect free' interpolated dataset over the domain of interest.

         We then extract the longest time period in our spatially interpolated dataset
         over which there are no data gaps
         (gaps can appear if a time point in the middle of the record failed the minimum requirement for spatial interpolation,
         which is why we cannot simply use the timespan of the observational record itself).
    }

    \item {
        \textbf{Step 3: Principal component analysis}

        We decompose the interpolated observational network based into three components:

        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Global-, annual-mean concentrations
            \item Relative seasonality at each latitude (i.e. seasonality at each latitude, divided by the global-, annual-mean)
            \item Latitudinal gradient components
        \end{enumerate}

        The latitudinal gradient components are a set of empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs, a function of latitude only)
        and principal components (PCs, a function of year only)
        derived using principal component analysis (PCA).
        The number of EOFs and PCs retained is either one or two, depending on the gas.

        The key to this decomposition is that the latitudinal gradient term has a spatial (area-weighted) mean of zero
        and that the seasonality term has an annual-mean of zero
        so that they can be added to the global- annual-mean term later (see Step 6)
        without affecting the corresponding global- annual-mean values.
    }

    \item {
        \textbf{Step 4: Extend}

        We extend the global-, annual-mean and latitudinal gradient PC(s) back to year 1.

        The global-, annual-mean is extended using ice cores where available,
        otherwise based on pre-industrial values from the literature and interpolation.

        The latitudinal gradient PC(s) are extended in one of two ways.
        The first is based on historical emissions, driven by a regression between emissions and the PC(s).
        The second is simple linear or constant extrapolation.
    }

    \item {
        \textbf{Step 5: Prepare the components to be combined}

        The global-, annual-mean, seasonality and latitudinal gradient components must be prepared before they can be combined.

        The first step is to to put all components on the same time axis.
        This introduces the first subtlety: while our approach is based on a timeseries of global-, annual-mean concentrations,
        this timeseries has to be interpolated to monthly timesteps
        in order to avoid the change from year to year resulting in step changes between December of one year and January of the next.
        The same interpolation requirement applies to the latitudinal gradient PC(s)
        (which are initially calculated on a yearly timestep, see step 3).
        The following calculations are therefore performed:

        \begin{enumerate}
            \item The global-, annual-mean is converted into a global-, monthly-mean using mean-preserving interpolation
            \item The relative seasonality is converted into an absolute seasonality over all time by multiplying it by the extended global-, annual-mean
            \item The latitudinal gradient PCs are converted to monthly steps using mean-preserving interpolation
            \item The latitudinal gradient is recovered by taking the product of the EOFs and the extended PCs
        \end{enumerate}
    }

    \item {
        \textbf{Step 6: Generate native resolution}

        We calculate the native resolution dataset value from the components, using the equation:

        \begin{equation}
            \label{eq:native-resolution-sum}
            gn(y, m, l) = gm(y, m) + lg(y, m, l) + s(y, m, l)
        \end{equation}
        where $gn$ is the native resolution dataset value
        (`gn' is the `grid label' used for such output on ESGF, so we re-use it here for convenience and consistency with the published output, see Section \ref{ssec:prep-for-esgf}),
        $y$ is the year in which the value applies,
        $m$ is the month in which the value applies,
        $l$ is the latitude at which the value applies,
        $gm$ is the global-, annual-mean derived value (this is broadcast across all latitudes during the sum),
        $lg$ is the latitudinal gradient contribution
        and $s$ is the seasonality contribution.
        As stated in Step 3, the key to this sum is that $lg$ has an area-weighted spatial mean of zero
        and $s$ has a temporal mean of zero
        so that the sum does not change the global- annual-mean value we started with.
    }

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Step 3: Principal component analysis}
\label{sssec:methods-n2o-pca}

We then take our spatially complete dataset and perform principal component analysis
following the general approach described in Section \ref{ssec:methods-general-approach}.
The specifics of this step are described in the rest of this Section (Section \ref{sssec:methods-n2o-pca}).

% \subsubsubsubsection{zonal mean}

The first step is to calculate the longitudinal (zonal) mean.
This is done using an unweighted mean because each grid cell has the same area for a given latitude.

% \subsubsubsubsection{global-, annual-mean}

The second step is to calculate the global-, annual-mean.
We do this by first calculating an area-weighted mean, then the annual-mean.
We weight each month equally.
Weighting the months by their length would make a minor difference,
but requires assuming that the processed data is truly representative of the entire month.
We don't make that assumption because our workflow can include processing of point measurements,
i.e. measurements taken at a particular point in time,
and grouping them in months is a necessary processing step
that should not be taken to mean
that the monthly values robustly represent the average over the entire month, given the month's length.

% \subsubsubsubsection{latitudinal gradient}

The third step is to calculate our latitudinal gradient.
We do this by first calculating the residuals at each latitude,
i.e. the difference between the annual-mean of the longitudinal mean and the global-, annual-mean.
On these residuals, we then perform principal component analysis.
We do this by using numpy's [TODO ref] singular value decomposition function (numpy.linalg.svd [TODO figure out monospace font]).
We translate the singular value decomposition output into two parts.
The first is our empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs) ([TODO check name in M17] referred to as principal components in M17).
These quantify the spatial, in this case latitudinal, variation in the dataset and are a function of latitude only.
The second is our principal components (PCs) ([TODO check name in M17] referred to as principal component scores in M17).
These quantify the temporal variation in the dataset and are a function of year only.
If all EOFs are retained, then the product of the EOFs and the PCs
reproduces the residuals we started with exactly.
However, following \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}
we only retain the first two EOFs as these are sufficient
to explain almost all of the variance.

% \subsubsubsubsection{seasonality}

The fourth step is to calculate our seasonality.
We do this by first calculating the residuals at each time and latitude.
The first component in this calculation is our longitudinal mean (dimensions (year, month, lat)).
The second component is the annual-mean at each latitude,
interpolated to monthly timesteps using mean-preserving interpolation, see Section \ref{sssec:methods-n2o-process-components}
(as a result, this data also has dimensions (year, month, lat)).
The interpolation to monthly timesteps is necessary
so that the residuals do not include step changes at each year boundary
(i.e. transition from December of year $Y$ to January of year $Y + 1$).
We then calculate the residuals by subtracting the monthly-interpolated
annual-mean at each latitude from the longitudinal mean.
The seasonality is the average of these residuals over all available years
and thus has dimensions (month, lat).

The last step is to construct the inputs needed for Equation \ref{eq:native-resolution-sum}
from the temporally extended components.

% \subsubsubsubsection{Interpolating global- annual-mean to monthly timesteps}
We start with the global-, annual-mean term, $gm(y, m)$.
This must be interpolated onto monthly timesteps.
This interpolation must be mean-preserving, i.e. the mean of the interpolated values must match the mean of the input values (over the same time bounds),
so that that the interpolated timeseries stays consistent with the global- annual-mean values.
We do this using the Lai-Kaplan algorithm \citep{lai-kaplan}.
We additionally use the Rymes-Meyers algorithm \citep{rymes-meyers}
when we need to ensure that interpolated values remain within specified bounds.
(Even with these algorithms,
the mean-preserving interpolation remains one of the most computationally expensive steps in the whole process).

% \subsubsubsubsection{Constructing seasonality over time}

Next we calculate the seasonality term, $s(y, m, l)$.
This is simply the product of the relative seasonality and the global-, annual-mean concentrations
(not the monthly interpolation though:
the seasonality already includes a monthly dimension
so using the monthly interpolation would lead to an absolute seasonality
which does not have an annual-mean of zero,
breaking the key requirement of the seasonality term).

% \subsubsubsubsection{Constructing latitudinal gradient over time (including interpolating PCs to monthly timesteps)}

Finally we calculate the latitudinal gradient term, $lg(y, m, l)$.
Like for the global-, annual-mean,
to avoid jumps at year boundaries, we must first interpolate the PCs to monthly timesteps
(again using mean-preserving interpolation).
Then the full latitudinal gradient is calculated
by taking the product of the temporally extended, monthly interpolated PCs and the EOFs.

% \subsubsubsubsection{seasonality}

The fourth step is to calculate our seasonality.
The calculation of the seasonality is the same as for
N_2O (Section \ref{sssec:methods-co2-pca-derived-components}).

On top of this seasonality, we also calculate EOFs and PCs for the change in seasonality over time.
These are calculated by taking the seasonality and residuals from the previous step.
We then calculate the difference between these two, which we call `seasonality anomalies'.
We then perform singular value decomposition on these seasonality anomalies
(this is done by stacking the data
so it has one dimension which is year
while the other dimension combines latitude and month
so that the singular value decomposition has a 2D dataset to work with).
As for the latitudinal gradient, we convert the singular value decomposition results into EOFs and PCs.

However, in the case of seasonality, while the PCs are still only a function of year,
the EOFs have dimensions (latitude, month).
For the seasonality change, again following \cite{meinshausen_historical_2017},
we retain only the first EOF.
This captures around half of the variance in the seasonality change,
with a single EOF being a pragmatic choice given that subsequent EOFs only explain 10\% or less of the variance
so an unworkably large number would be required to capture a large fraction of the variance.

\label{sssec:methods-co2-backward-extension}

The second PC is much simpler.
It is simply linearly interpolated between its value at the start of
the observational period, in our case 1981, and zero in 1850.
It is kept constant at zero before 1850.

% \subsubsubsubsection{Global- annual-mean}

To extend the global- annual-mean back in time, we rely on the
[TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] and ice core values from [TODO cite Menking et al 2025].

We start with the [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record].
We use all data from [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] which is not already covered in the observational record.
In practice this means that we use [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] from 1959 to 1980.
There is an offset of around 1~ppm between the [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record]
and the global- annual-mean we derive from the observational network
in the time period in which they overlap.
To avoid introducing an artificial step in the output,
we harmonise the [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] data to the observational network
i.e. add a declining offset (with the decline set to reach zero over 100 years)
to ensure that the [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] data and the observational network
derived global- annual-mean agree in 1981 - their first overlap year.
This is crude and a clear area for further investigation in future work ([TODO forward ref discussion]).

We then use data from [TODO cite Menking et al 2025] to extend back to year 1.
We use the spline-interpolated data from [TODO cite Menking et al 2025],
removing the need for us to make our own spline interpolation choices (unlike \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}).
As for the [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] data,
there is a small offset, albeit of only around 0.5~ppm in this case,
between the [TODO cite Menking et al 2025]
and the value implied by our global- annual-mean and latitudinal gradient
in the time period in which they overlap.
Like for the [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] data,
to avoid introducing an artificial step in the output,
we harmonise the [TODO cite Menking et al 2025] data.
We then calculate the temporally extended global- annual-mean such that,
when it is combined with the (now temporally extended) latitudinal gradient,
our output dataset matches the harmonised [TODO cite Menking et al 2025]
in the latitudinal bin in which the ice cores were taken exactly
(this is the -67.5\textdegree~bin on our native grid).

% \subsubsubsubsection{Seasonality}

Unlike N_2O, further processing of the seasonality is required to
extend the seasonality change PC back in time.

Following \cite{meinshausen_historical_2017},
the PC is extended by performing a linear regression against a composite of global-mean surface air temperatures from [TODO cite HadCRUT]
and our (now extended) global- annual-mean CO_2 concentrations (Equation \ref{eq:co2-pc-composite-regressor}):

\begin{align}
    \label{eq:co2-pc-composite-regressor}
    r(y) &= \frac{t'(y) + c'(y)}{4} + \frac{t'(y) \cdot c'(y)}{2} \\
    t'(y) &= \frac{t(y) - t_r}{t_n - t_r}  \\
    c'(y) &= \frac{c(y) - c_r}{c_n - c_r}  \\
\end{align}
where $r(y)$ is the composite timeseries against which we regress,
$t(y)$ is global- annual-mean temperature after applying a 15-year centred smoothing with at least 5 points required for the smoothed value to be considered valid,
$c(y)$ is global- annual-mean CO_2 concentrations,
$_r$ indicates that the quantity is a mean over the reference period (1850-1880 in this study, following \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}),
and $_n$ indicates that the quantity is a mean over the normalisation period (2000-2010 in this study, following \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}).

Using this regression, we extend the PC back to 1850,
the start of [TODO cite HadCRUT]'s dataset.
Before 1850, we keep the PC value constant.

we do a mean-preserving interpolation of the global-, annual-mean

by taking the product of the temporally extended PC and the EOF.
This seasonality change is then added onto our `base seasonality' to give the full seasonality term.

As for N_2O (Section \ref{sssec:methods-n2o-process-components}),
the last step is to calculate the latitudinal gradient term, $lg(y, m, l)$.

\subsubsection{Step 4: Extend}
\label{sssec:methods-ch4-extending-components-in-time}

% \subsubsubsubsection{Latitudinal gradient}

Following \cite{meinshausen_historical_2017},
the first PC is initially extended by performing a linear regression against CH_4 emissions of a geological origin
(as opposed to CH_4 emissions that originate in the active biosphere)
from the [TODO cite PRIMAP version] dataset.
Using this regression, we extend the first PC back to the latest year
in which we have observations from ice cores in both the northern and southern hemispheres,
in our case 1948.

For the period including and before 1948, we use ice core information to optimise the first PC.
We use ice core values from [TODO cite Law Dome] for the southern hemisphere
and [TODO cite Neem] for the northern hemisphere.
We do not use the values from [TODO cite Menking et al 2025] because of a processing error
([TODO forward ref discussion of this, basically say that it was an error,
but doesn't affect CMIP datasets in a material way
because of how similar the Law Dome and Menking data is,
but users of CH_4 data before 1850 should be aware and may wish to make other choices]).
We smooth the ice core data from [TODO cite Law Dome] following the method used in \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}.
To each data point in the [TODO cite Law Dome] dataset,
we add random noise.
The noise is normally distributed.
In the value axis, it has a standard deviation of 3~ppb.
In the time axis, the standard deviation increases as the age of the observation increases
according to the equation $\sigma = 0.025 \times (2024 - y)$, where $y$ is the year to which the observation applies.
For each year between the start and end of the [TODO cite Law Dome] dataset,
we pick at least four points either side of that year and a maximum of ten points either side of that year.
If there are four or more points within 100 years of the year of interest
(on a given side, i.e. before or after the year of interest),
the points that are closest to the year for which we are trying to generate a value are used (on that side).
If there are fewer than four points within 100 years of the window of interest
(on a given side, i.e. before or after the year of interest),
then the nearest four points on that side are used, irrespective of how far away they are from the year of interest.
Based on the selected points, a quantile regression with a third order polynomial is performed.
The inferred value for the year of interest is then taken from this regression.
This process is repeated over all years, and the entire process is then repeated 250 times.
The smoothed dataset is then the median over these 250 repeats.

The first PC is then linearly interpolated between the years in which we have observations for both ice cores.

the ice core extension

The second PC is simply kept constant before the start of the observational period.

% \subsubsubsubsection{Global- annual-mean}

To extend the global- annual-mean back in time,

As for the smoothed [TODO cite Law Dome] data,

As a final check, we verify that the values implied by our global- annual-mean and latitudinal gradient

\subsubsection{Step 4: Extend}
\label{sssec:methods-cfc12-extending-components-in-time}

% \subsubsubsubsection{Latitudinal gradient}

We extend the latitudinal gradient PC (as we retain only one)
back in time by using a regression against total emissions
from the SSP2-4.5 scenario (which includes the historical period) in the RCMIP v5.1.0 emissions dataset \citep{nicholls_rcmip_2020}.
Using this regression, we then extend the PC back to 1750,
the first year reported in the RCMIP v5.1.0 emissions dataset.
Before 1750, we keep the PC constant.

% \subsubsubsubsection{Global- annual-mean}

The global-, annual-mean is calculated in two steps.
First, we set the global-, annual-mean over the period covered by (potentially) multiple input data sources.
For most gases, we use the global-, annual-mean from a specific source
rather than the global-, annual-mean derived from the observational network
(Table \ref{tab:cfc12-like-per-gas}).
For CF_4, C_2F_6 and C_3F_8, we use the global-, annual-mean from \citet{trudinger_2016}.
However, this does not extend as close to the present-day as our observation network dataset does,
so we create a composite.
For the time period covered by \citet{trudinger_2016}, but not by our observation network global-mean,
we use harmonised \citet{trudinger_2016} data.
We harmonise the \citet{trudinger_2016} data to the global-, annual-mean derived from the observational network
in the first year for which we have data from the observational network
(adding an offset which would decline linearly to zero over a 100 year period).
For the rest of the time period, we use the global-, annual-mean derived from the observational network.
For the remaining gases, we use the global-, annual-mean derived from the observational network
(Table \ref{tab:cfc12-like-per-gas}).

Second, for all gases, we extend the global-, annual-mean back in time based on information about the gas' pre-industrial value and when this pre-industrial value should be reached
(Table \ref{tab:cfc12-like-per-gas}).
We then fill the gap between the pre-industrial value (assumed constant for all time points before the pre-industrial fitting year)
and the global-, annual-mean from the previous step following the equation:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cfc12-like-global-mean-gap-fill}
    c(y) = a \left( \frac{y - y_{pi}}{w} \right)^4 + b \left( \frac{y - y_{pi}}{w} \right)^2
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the global-, annual-mean concentration,
$y$ is the year in which the value applies,
$y_{pi}$ is the pre-industrial year
and $w$ is half the width of the gap.

We fit this function to the data from the three years immediately before the gap
and up to three years immediately after it, requiring that $a \geq 0$ and $b \geq 0$.
Having only even powers of $y - y_{pi}$, with non-negative coefficients,
means that the fit is flat at the pre-industrial year
and rises monotonically away from it,
which ensures that concentrations of these gases only start to rise after the pre-industrial year.
Where this fit leads to values that are not within the range of values seen on either side of the gap
(i.e. there are oscillations in the output)
we instead interpolate through the same points with a quadratic and, failing that, a cubic spline.

\subsection{Derived output grids}
\label{ssec:methods-derived-grids}

For each gas, we apply Equation \ref{eq:native-resolution-sum}
to create a gridded dataset on our 15\textdegree~latitudinal (spatial), monthly (temporal) `native resolution' grid.
From this native grid output, we then derive four other output products:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item hemispheric (spatial) monthly (temporal) grid
    \item global-mean (spatial) monthly (temporal) grid
    \item hemispheric (spatial) yearly (temporal) grid
    \item global-mean (spatial) yearly (temporal) grid
\end{enumerate}

These outputs are trivially derived by taking means in the relevant axes
(where the spatial means are area-weighted).
The outputs are then formatted before being published on the ESGF for distribution to modelling teams.

We also have the ability to produce higher-resolution spatial grids
by applying mean-preserving interpolation to the latitudinal axis of the native grid output.
Arbitrary latitudinal resolution can be produced by applying this technique.
%   See https://github.com/climate-resource/CMIP-GHG-Concentration-Generation/blob/v0.2.0/src/local/mean_preserving_interpolation.py
%   if anyone ever needs these outputs.
As a result of the processing time, data storage requirements and lack of use in CMIP6,
we have not produced the 0.5\textdegree~latitudinal grid product as part of our `standard' offering this time.

\subsection{Equivalent species}
\label{ssec:methods-equivalent-species}

Given that the majority, it not all, ESMs will not want the computational expense of including 43 greenhouse gas tracers,
we also produce the equivalent species provided in \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}.
These equivalent species allow modelling teams to pick one of three options when running:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Use all GHGs individually: modelling teams can pick which of the 43 provided GHGs to run with. Note that running with any of the 43 GHGs missing means that some GHG radiative effects will be missing from the simulation
    \item Use CO_2, CH_4, N_2O, CFC12 and CFC-11 equivalent: CFC-11 captures the equivalent effect of all GHGs other than CO_2, CH_4, N_2O and CFC12.
    \item Use CO_2, CH_4, N_2O, CFC12 equivalent and HFC134a equivalent: CFC12 captures the equivalent effect of all ozone-depleting substances (namely CFC-11, CFC12, CFC-113, CFC-114, CFC-115, CCl_4, CH_2Cl_2, CH_3Br, CH_3CCl_3, CH_3Cl, CHCl_3, Halon-1211, Halon-1301, Halon-2402, HCFC-141b, HCFC-142b, HCFC-22) while all other fluorinated gases are included in HFC134a (namely CF_4, C_2F_6, C_3F_8, cC_4F_8, C_4F_10, C_5F_12, C_6F_14, C_7F_16, C_8F_18, HFC-23, HFC-32, HFC-125, HFC134a, HFC-143a, HFC-152a, HFC-227ea, HFC-236fa, HFC-245fa, HFC-365mfc, HFC-4310mee, NF_3, SF_6, SO_2F_2)
\end{enumerate}

The equivalence is calculated following the `full equivalence' definition given in \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}.
[TODO in the citation, add see their Equation 7].
We use the radiative efficiencies from the last IPCC Assessment Report,
specifically the radiative efficiencies given in Table 7.SM.6 of the Working Group 1 Chapter 7 Supplementary Material [TODO citation].

These equivalent species are derived based on output that is already on the desired output grid,
rather than on the native grid (with further processing applied thereafter).
This avoids any consistency issues due to non-linearity in the processing operations,
such as from mean-preserving interpolation.

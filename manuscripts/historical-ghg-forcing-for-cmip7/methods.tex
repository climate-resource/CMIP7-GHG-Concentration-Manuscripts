\section{Methods}

To derive our dataset, we largely follow the methods described in \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}.
However, we have made changes in a number of places
so we describe the method in full,
while also highlighting places of clear divergence.

\subsection{General approach}

In line with \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017},
the general approach is to first create a dataset with monthly temporal resolution
and 15\textdegree latitudinal spatial resolution
(but no longitudinal resolution).
We refer to this as our `native resolution'.
From our native resolution, we can then create higher and lower resolution data products (see [TODO: forward refernence]).

To create our native resolution dataset, we sum three components:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Global-, annual-mean concentrations
  \item Seasonal variations
  \item Latitudinal gradient
\end{enumerate}

In order to take this sum,
all three components must already have the same time dimension
(monthly timesteps in our case).
This introduces the first subtlety: while our approach is based on a timeseries of global-, annual-mean concentrations,
this timeseries has to be interpolated to monthly timesteps
in order to avoid the change from year to year resulting in step changes between December of one year and January of the next.
The same interpolation requirement applies to the latitudinal gradient
(which is initially calculated on a yearly timestep, see [TODO forward ref]).

In addition to the time dimension consistency requirement, seasonality can vary by latitude.
As a result, the latitudinal variation of seasonality must be calculated
(i.e. seasonality must have a "latitude" dimension)
before taking the sum.

Thus, in practice, we calculate each dataset according to equation \ref{eq:native-resolution-sum}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:native-resolution-sum}
    gn(y, m, l) = gm(y, m) + lg(y, m, l) + s(y, m, l)
\end{equation}
where $gn$ is the native resolution dataset value
(`gn' is the `grid label' used for such output on ESGF, so we re-use it here for convenience and consistency with the published output, see [TODO forward ref]),
$y$ is the year in which the value applies,
$m$ is the month in which the value applies,
$l$ is the latitude at which the value applies,
$gm$ is the global-, annual-mean derived value (and is broadcast across all latitudes),
$lg$ is the latitudinal gradient contribution
and $s$ is the seasonality contribution.

The key choices are about how we arrive at $gm$, $lg$ and $s$ for each greenhouse gas species in our dataset.
We describe the different methods in the following sections.

\subsection{Calculating the general components}

\subsubsection{CO_2}

CO_2 has the longest observational record of all the greenhouse gases included in our dataset.
It is also the largest driver of climate change to date [TODO citation], hence has the most available observations.
Finally, atmospheric CO_2 concentrations are driven by the carbon cycle,
which led to the introduction of extra complexity in the calculation of the input components,
with the aim of better representing the variations seen in the observational record.

We begin by collecting observations of surface CO_2 concentrations from the NOAA observational networks.
We bin these observations into 15\textdegree latitudinal, 60\textdegree longitudinal and monthly temporal bins.
Within each bin, we firstly take the mean of all measurements that come from the same network
(where, for NOAA, we treat the in-situ and surface flask measurements as separate networks),
station and measurement method.
[TODO figure out what different measurement methods are]
As a result, each network, station and measurement method gets an equal weight in each bin,
irrespective of how many measurements that network-station-measurement made in the bin.
Then, we take the average over network, station and measurement method
to get a single value in each bin.

The surface observational network does not cover the entire globe [TODO: figure],
hence the next step is to interpolate the available measurements to get complete spatial coverage.
We specify that at least five data points are required for us to do spatial interpolation
and we must have data points in both the most northern and most southern latitudinal box.
This is clearly a relatively low data requirement,
but higher thresholds result in a significant amount of the observational record being discarded.
More robust interpolation methods are an area for future work [TODO: forward ref].
We then take the longest (in time) dataset that is continuous.
In this dataset, that meant that we used data from 1981 to 2023 (both inclusive).

% \subsubsubsection{Spatial interpolation}

We use 2D interpolation
to go from the coverage provided by the underlying observational networks
to complete global coverage.
The 2D interpolation is linear barycentric interpolation,
as implemented in scipy's griddata function [TODO ref scipy].
Before interpolating, we put the data on a grid that is `wrapped around' to avoid edge effects.
In practice, this means that we duplicate the data three times
and place these three copies next to each other in the longitudinal direction.
As a result, each point in the central `copy' `sees' its closest neighbours, irrespective of its longitude.
After interpolating, we throw away the outer `copies'
and are left with an `edge effect free' interpolated dataset over the domain of interest.

% \subsubsubsection{PCA}

We then take our spatially complete dataset and perform principal component analysis.

% \subsubsubsubsection{zonal mean}

The first step is to calculate the longitudinal (zonal) mean.
This is done using a basic mean as each grid cell has the same area for a given latitude.

% \subsubsubsubsection{global-, annual-mean}

The second step is to calculate the global-, annual-mean.
We do this using an area-weighted mean
and then an annual-mean.
We weight each month equally.
Weighting the months by their length would make a minor difference,
but requires assuming that the processed data is truly representative of the entire month.
We don't make that assumption because we often have point measurements
i.e. measurements taken at a particular point in time
and grouping them in months is a necessary processing step
that should not be taken to mean
that the monthly values robustly represent the average over the entire month, given the month's length.

% \subsubsubsubsection{latitudinal gradient}

The third step is to calculate our latitudinal gradient.
We do this by first calculating the residuals at each latitude,
i.e. the difference between the annual-mean of the longitudinal mean and the global-, annual-mean.
On these residuals, we then perform principal component analysis.
We do this by using numpy's [TODO ref] singular value decomposition function (numpy.linalg.svd [TODO figure out monospace font]).
We translate the singular value decomposition output into two parts.
The first is our empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs) ([TODO check name in M17] referred to as principal components in M17).
These quantify the spatial, in this case latitudinal, variation in the dataset (and are a function of latitude only).
The second is our principal components (PCs) ([TODO check name in M17] referred to as principal component scores in M17).
These quantify the temporal variation in the dataset (and are a function of year only).
If all EOFs are retained, then the product of the EOFs and the PCs
reproduces the residuals we started with exactly.
However, following \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017}
we only retain the first two EOFs as these are sufficient
to reproduce the residuals to within 1~ppm CO_2 (and are usually much closer than this).

% \subsubsubsubsection{seasonality}

The fourth step is to calculate our seasonality.
We do this by first calculating the residuals at each time and latitude.
The first component in this calculation is our longitudinal mean (dimensions (year, month, lat)).
The second component is the annual-mean at each latitude,
interpolated to monthly timesteps (as a result, this data also has dimensions (year, month, lat)).
We then calculate the residuals by subtracting the monthly-interpolated annual-mean at each latitude
from the longitudinal mean.
The interpolation to monthly timesteps is necessary
so that the residuals do not include step changes at each year boundary
(i.e. transition from December of year $Y$ to January of year $Y + 1$).
The seasonality is the average of these residuals over all available years
and thus has dimensions (month, lat).

On top of this seasonality, we also calculate EOFs and PCs for the change in seasonality over time.
These are calculated by taking the seasonality and residuals from the previous step.
We then calculate the difference between these two, which we call `seasonality anomalies'.
We then perform singular value decomposition on these seasonality anomalies
(this is done by stacking the data so it has one dimension which is year
and the latitude and month dimensions are stacked into a single dimension
so that the singular value decomposition has a 2D dataset to work with).
As for the latitudinal gradient, we convert the singular value decomposition results
into EOFs and PCs.
However, in the case of seasonality, while the PCs are still only a function of year,
the EOFs have dimensions (latitude, month).
For the seasonality change, again following \cite{meinshausen_historical_2017},
we retain only the first EOF (although this results in larger differences, up to 2~ppm,
between the seasonality anomalies and the EOF-based reproduction
than was the case for the EOF-based latitudinal gradient).

At this point we have, or can construct, all the required input components
over the time period covered by the observational network.
The last step is to extend these intermediate data products
so that we can produce the required input components
over the entire time period included in the output dataset.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item NOAA
  \item Seasonality (year, month, latitude)
  \item Latitudinal gradient (year, month, latitude)
\end{enumerate}

For all GHGs, we need three things:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Global-mean concentration with monthly temporal resolution (explain somewhere why this doesn't work with annual-mean resolution because of the jumps you get from December to January)
  \item Seasonality (year, month, latitude)
  \item Latitudinal gradient (year, month, latitude)
\end{enumerate}

The trick is how we get these for different gases.
Breaks down into six cases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item CO_2
    \item CH_4
    \item N_2O
    \item SF_6-like
    \item C_4F_10-like
    \item C_8F_18-like
\end{enumerate}

CO_2:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Bin obs
    \item Interpolate/extrapolate to complete spatial coverage
    \item Do PCA to get global-, annual-mean, seasonality and latitudinal gradient over the observational era with sufficient data
    \item Extend latitudinal gradient PCs back in time using fossil emissions
    \item Extend global-mean back in time (depends on the previous step)
    \item Extend seasonality back in time using our funny temperature-concentration composite
    \item Put it all back together to get the pieces for gridding
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Get annual-mean monthly
        \item Calculate seasonality over time (i.e. base seasonality plus seasonality change)
        \item Interpolate latitudinal gradient PCs to monthly
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

CH_4:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Bin obs
    \item Interpolate/extrapolate to complete spatial coverage
    \item Do PCA to get global-, annual-mean, seasonality and latitudinal gradient over the observational era with sufficient data
    \item Extend latitudinal gradient PCs back in time using fossil emissions
    \item Extend global-mean back in time (depends on the previous step)
    \item Put it all back together to get the pieces for gridding
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Get annual-mean monthly
        \item Calculate seasonality over time (i.e. multiply base seasonality by global-mean values)
        \item Interpolate latitudinal gradient PCs to monthly
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

N_2O:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Bin obs
    \item Interpolate/extrapolate to complete spatial coverage
    \item Do PCA to get global-, annual-mean, seasonality and latitudinal gradient over the observational era with sufficient data
    \item Extend latitudinal gradient PCs back in time using total emissions
    \item Extend global-mean back in time (depends on the previous step)
    \item Put it all back together to get the pieces for gridding
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Get annual-mean monthly
        \item Calculate seasonality over time (i.e. multiply base seasonality by global-mean values)
        \item Interpolate latitudinal gradient PCs to monthly
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

SF_6:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Bin obs
    \item Interpolate/extrapolate to complete spatial coverage
    \item Do PCA to get global-, annual-mean, seasonality and latitudinal gradient over the observational era with sufficient data
    \item Extend latitudinal gradient PCs back in time using total emissions
    \item Extend global-mean back in time using pre-industrial value plus interpolation
    \item Put it all back together to get the pieces for gridding
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Get annual-mean monthly
        \item Calculate seasonality over time (i.e. multiply base seasonality by global-mean values)
        \item Interpolate latitudinal gradient PCs to monthly
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

C_4F_10:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Get latitudinal gradient
    \item Put it all back together to get the pieces for gridding
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Get annual-mean monthly
        \item Assume zero seasonality
        \item Interpolate latitudinal gradient PCs to monthly
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

C_8F_18:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item No new data, so just use CMIP6 pieces (historical plus ssp245, minor gas so different choices make negligible difference [TODO quantify])
    \item Put it all back together to get the pieces for gridding
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Get annual-mean monthly
        \item Assume zero seasonality
        \item Interpolate latitudinal gradient PCs to monthly
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

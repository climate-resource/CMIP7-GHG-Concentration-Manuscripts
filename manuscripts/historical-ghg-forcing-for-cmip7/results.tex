\subsection{Radiative forcing comparisons}
\label{ssec:results-radiative-forcing-comparisons}

Throughout this section, we provide comparisons between the output CMIP7 datasets and the CMIP6 datasets.
We provide these comparisons both in units of concentration as well as in units of W/m2.
It must be noted that the comparisons in units of W/m2 are an approximation.
To approximately convert from atmospheric concentrations to radiative forcing
we use linear radiative efficiencies from \citet{IPCC_2021_WGI_Ch_7_SM}.
This allows us to provide this conversion for all concentrations over all time,
rather than having to arbitrarily pick a particular point in time as the reference point
(and therefore forcing radiative forcing differencs for that point in time to be zero).
This approximation matches or overestimates the true (effective) radiative forcing,
because true radiative forcing is reduced as each wavelength band saturates as greenhouse gas concentrations increase.
As a result, the approximate radiative forcing differences shown here are the `worst case' differences i.e. the most conservative choice when considering differences.
Users, particularly modelling teams, should note that they may see smaller effects than this
when they use more accurate conversions from concentrations to (effective) radiative forcing
or apply these concentrations in their own radiative balance calculations.

\subsection{CO_2}
\label{ssec:results-co2}

Atmospheric CO2 concentrations continue to trend upwards approximately linearly
with a strong seasonal cycle in the northern hemisphere (Figure \ref{fig:results-co2}).
The output dataset matches NOAA's global-mean monthly and trend timeseries [TODO citation] to within approximately 1~ppm.
The output dataset also reproduces the absolute values and seasonal cycle seen in NOAA's Mauna Loa timeseries [TODO citation]
to within approximately 1~ppm once Maunoa Loa's latitude is taken into account.
While these datasets are not totally independent of our dataset (NOAA's network is used as an input),
this provides confidence that the data processing and statistical modelling we have performed are not introducing artefacts in the outputs
and that the method we have used allows us to reproduce the key spatial and temporal features of the input datasets.

The dataset has changed relatively little from the CMIP6 dataset, despite the completely new implementation of the methods.
Over the period used for CMIP modelling, 1850 onwards, the maximum deviation is 2~ppm, which is approximately 0.02~W/m2 in radiative forcing terms.
Over the length of the dataset, the maximum deviation is 4~ppm in around year 200 (\textasciitilde{}0.05~ W/m2).
The changes over the period where we use the observation network, i.e. from 1981 onwards, are approximately zero.

The changes from CMIP6 are driven by how we extend the dataset back in time before the availability of the observation network.
Rather than relying on ice cores and performing our own smoothing, as was done in \citet{meinshausen_historical_2017},
we instead used the [TODO cite Mauna Loa merged record] and smoothed ice core values from [TODO cite Menking et al 2025].
These datasets produce different results, most notably removing an \textasciitilde{}2~ppm dip in the CMIP6 dataset around 1940
and a \textasciitilde{}2~ppm spike in the CMIP6 dataset around 1970.

\subsection{CFC-12 and similarly processed species}
\label{ssec:results-cfc12-like}

Atmospheric CFC12 concentrations continue to drop at an accelerating rate
with a clear seasonal cycle (Figure \ref{fig:results-cfc12}).
The output is a clear middle-ground between the NOAA [TODO citation] and AGAGE [TODO citation] networks,
which disagree by about 5~ppt.
The output dataset reproduces the seasonal cycle and latitudinal gradient seen in the input datasets,
including the weakening, and perhaps even reversing, latitudinal gradient.
As for CO_2, CH_4 and N_2O,
these results provide confidence that the data processing and statistical modelling we have performed are not introducing artefacts in the outputs
and that the method we have used allows us to reproduce the key spatial and temporal features of the input datasets.

The dataset has changed little from the CMIP6 dataset.
Over the length of the dataset, the maximum deviation is 5~ppt in around 1980 (\textasciitilde{}0.001~ W/m2).
The changes from CMIP6 are driven by updates to the input datasets,
which are based on an updated assessment by the World Meteorological Organisation [WMO, cite Daniel et al.].

\subsection{Equivalent species}
\label{ssec:results-equivalent-species}

The equivalent species summarise the changes across the minor greenhouse gases.
Atmospheric CFC12 equivalent concentrations plateaued through the 2000s and have been declining at an accelerating rate since around 2010 (Figure \ref{fig:results-cfc12eq}),
following the decline in the ozone-depleting substances they represent.
In contrast, atmospheric HFC-134a equivalent concentrations continue to rise rapidly,
roughly quadrupling since 2000 (Figure \ref{fig:results-hfc134aeq}).

The CFC12 equivalent dataset is lower than the CMIP6 dataset by up to 44~ppt (approximately 4\%, \textasciitilde{}0.016~W/m2) in the observational period
and by approximately 8~ppt (\textasciitilde{}0.003~W/m2) in the pre-industrial period.
These differences are driven by the radiative efficiencies used to calculate the equivalence,
rather than by changes in the concentrations of the underlying gases.
CMIP6 used the radiative efficiencies from the IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report [TODO citation, AR5 WG1 Ch. 8 Appendix 8.A],
whereas we use those from the Sixth Assessment Report (Section \ref{ssec:methods-equivalent-species}).
Recalculating the CMIP6 equivalent species from the CMIP6 concentrations of the individual gases
using the Fifth Assessment Report radiative efficiencies reproduces the published CMIP6 equivalent species exactly (to within 0.001~ppt in every year),
which confirms that the CMIP6 equivalent species include each gas once and only once.
If the CMIP6 CFC12 equivalent is instead recalculated using the Sixth Assessment Report radiative efficiencies,
the difference from our dataset reduces to at most 8~ppt (\textasciitilde{}0.003~W/m2) over the length of the dataset
and becomes effectively zero in the pre-industrial period.
For modelling teams who use the equivalent species, the radiative efficiency driven difference is real:
it brings the forcing from ozone-depleting substances in line with the latest assessment.

The HFC-134a equivalent dataset has changed very little from the CMIP6 dataset.
Over the length of the dataset, the maximum deviation is 3~ppt in around 1980 (\textasciitilde{}0.0005~W/m2).
Recalculating the CMIP6 HFC-134a equivalent with the Sixth Assessment Report radiative efficiencies removes the 1~ppt difference in the pre-industrial period,
leaving a maximum deviation of 3~ppt, driven by the updated concentrations of the individual HFCs.

The Indicators of Global Climate Change (IGCC) assessment \citep{forster_indicators_2026,smith_forcing_timeseries_2026}
also reports CFC12 and HFC-134a equivalent concentrations.
These are not comparable to our equivalent species, because they are based on a different group of gases
(IGCC's CFC12 equivalent includes eight ozone-depleting substances which are not in our dataset,
while IGCC's HFC-134a equivalent includes only the HFCs, i.e. none of the PFCs, SF_6, NF_3 or SO_2F_2)
and different radiative efficiencies \citep[those of][]{hodnebrog_2020}.
As a result, in 2022 IGCC's CFC12 equivalent is approximately 40~ppt higher than ours
and IGCC's HFC-134a equivalent is approximately 120~ppt lower than ours (Figures \ref{fig:results-cfc12eq} and \ref{fig:results-hfc134aeq}).
The CFC12 equivalent difference is driven mostly by the radiative efficiencies rather than by the extra gases.
Recalculating IGCC's CFC12 equivalent from IGCC's concentrations of the individual gases
shows that, of the 40~ppt difference in 2022,
approximately 35~ppt comes from the different radiative efficiencies,
approximately 6~ppt comes from the extra gases (half of which is CFC-13)
and the remaining approximately 1~ppt of the opposite sign comes from differences in the concentrations of the gases included in both.
The radiative efficiencies of \citet{hodnebrog_2020} and the Sixth Assessment Report are almost identical,
except for CFC-11 and CFC12, whose Sixth Assessment Report radiative efficiencies are approximately 12\% higher
[TODO check: because the Sixth Assessment Report includes tropospheric adjustments for these two gases].
As CFC12 is the reference gas for the equivalence,
its higher radiative efficiency reduces the weight given to every other gas except CFC-11 by approximately 11\%,
which lowers our CFC12 equivalent relative to IGCC's.
The largest single contribution is from HCFC-22 (approximately 18~ppt in 2022),
followed by CFC-113 (approximately 7~ppt) and CCl_4 (approximately 4~ppt).
We show them to make clear that equivalent species are only meaningful alongside their definition:
users should not mix equivalent species from different sources.

\subsection{A note on radiative forcing comparisons}
\label{ssec:results-radiative-forcing-comparisons-note}

Throughout this section, we provide comparisons between the output CMIP7 datasets and the CMIP6 datasets.
We provide these comparisons both in units of concentration as well as in units of W/m2.
It must be noted that the comparisons in units of W/m2 are an approximation.
To approximately convert from atmospheric concentrations to radiative forcing
% ZNTODO: zalue-check here
we use linear radiative efficiencies from \citet{IPCC_AR6-WG1-Ch-7-SM_2021}.
This allows us to provide this conversion for all concentrations over all time,
rather than having to arbitrarily pick a particular point in time as the reference point
(and therefore arbitrarily setting radiative forcing differencs for that point in time to zero).
This approximation matches or overestimates the true (effective) radiative forcing,
because true radiative forcing is reduced as each wavelength band saturates as greenhouse gas concentrations increase.
As a result, the approximate radiative forcing differences shown here are the `worst case' differences
i.e. the most conservative choice when considering differences.
Users, particularly modelling teams, should note that they may see smaller effects than this
when they use more accurate conversions from concentrations to (effective) radiative forcing
or apply these concentrations in their own radiative balance calculations.

The output dataset is also in line with UCI's quarterly timeseries \citep{simpson_ethane_2012}

which we retrieved from the data supplement of \citet{saunois_methane-budget_2025},
but is generally approximately 15~ppb higher.
The difference from the UCI dataset is partly explained by the fact that the UCI dataset is not based on a global sampling,
rather only atmospheric samples from the Pacific basin between approximately 71\textdegree N and 47\textdegree S \citep{blake_uci-network-readme_2010}.
The difference from the NOAA dataset is not completely unexpected.
While the NOAA dataset is not totally independent of our dataset (NOAA's network is used as an input),
our dataset also includes the AGAGE \citep{prinn_ale-gage-agage_2000,prinn_agage_2018} network data as an input
and we would thus expect differences (and similar differences appear in the CMIP6 dataset too).
The difference from the IGCC data is more interesting, given that IGCC also uses a both NOAA and AGAGE as input.
The IGCC data is, in the annual-mean, very close to the NOAA data.
% Given that the NOAA and AGAGE scales are negligible in their differences, we rule out scale as the source of this difference.
Our best estimate is that this points to a difference in how global-means are calculated from the sparse observation network.
This is definitely an area for further investigation
and may indicate that the method to go from raw observations to an estimate of the global-mean
is a key source of uncertainty/differences between datasets (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
Examining the differences from the UCI and NOAA data suggests
that our (and the CMIP6 dataset's) seasonal cycle may be slightly too strong.
This could be addressed by introducing a more sophisticated handling of CH4's seasonal cycle,
rather than the simple relative seasonality approach we have used.

\subsection{Equivalence datasets}
\label{ssec:results-equivalence-datasets}

The last datasets we consider are the equivalence datasets, CFC12-eq and HFC134a-eq.
As described in Section~\ref{ssec:methods-equivalent-species},
CFC12-eq approximates the equivalent effect of all ozone-depleting substances
while HFC134a-eq approximates the equivalent effect of all other fluorinated gases.
The results for CFC-11-eq, which captures the effect of all the gases in these two except CFC12,
are in Figure~\ref{fig:results-cfc11eq}.
For species by species comparisons, please see Appendix~\ref{app:results-figures}.

\subsubsection{CFC-12-eq}
\label{sssec:results-equivalence-datasets-cfc12eq}

% Checked (2026-10-08): our radiative efficiencies match AR6 Table 7.SM.7.
% The halving is CH3Cl: AR5 (used for CMIP6) has 0.01 W/m^2/ppb, AR6 has 0.005 W/m^2/ppb.

% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-last-10-years-trend", "unit": "ppt/yr", "upper": 0}
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-last-10-years-second-derivative", "unit": "ppt/yr/yr", "upper": 0}
CFC12-eq has continued to decline since 2015 at an accelerating rate (Figure~\ref{fig:results-cfc12eq}).
The seasonal cycle and latitudinal gradient is also clear for this equivalence species.
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-igcc-last-year", "unit": "ppt", "upper": -35, "lower": -45}
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-igcc-last-year-radiative-effect", "unit": "W/m^2", "upper": 0.02, "lower": 0.01}
Our dataset is around 40~ppt lower than the IGCC dataset (\textasciitilde{}0.015~W/m2).
This is driven by two factors: a) we use radiative efficiencies from the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report \citep[AR6,][]{IPCC_AR6-WG1-Ch-7-SM_2021}
whereas IGCC uses radiative efficiencies from \citet{hodnebrog_gwps-efficiencies_2020}
and b) the IGCC dataset contains gases which we do not include.
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-igcc-radiative-efficiency-share", "unit": "dimensionless", "upper": 0.95, "lower": 0.85}
Of these two, the difference in radiative efficiencies is the dominant one,
contributing approximately 90\% of the difference.
This is a key takeaway for users of these equivalent species:
their definition and calculation methodology matters
and the IGCC products are not calculated in the same way as our CMIP forcing datasets.
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-igcc-max-radiative-effect", "unit": "W/m^2", "upper": 0.02, "lower": 0.01}
Nonetheless, in approximate radiative forcing terms the difference is about 0.015~W/m2
so the distinction may not always be a crucial one to recognise.

The dataset has changed from the CMIP6 dataset.
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-cmip6-1995-on", "unit": "ppt", "upper": 45, "lower": 35}
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-cmip6-1995-on-radiative-effect", "unit": "W/m^2", "upper": 0.0175, "lower": 0.0125}
Over the length of the dataset, the maximum deviation is 40~ppt from 1995 onwards (\textasciitilde{}0.015~W/m2).
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-cmip6-radiative-efficiency-share", "unit": "dimensionless", "lower": 0.5}
The changes from CMIP6 are driven by the use of updated radiative efficiencies.
The CMIP6 dataset was calculated using radiative efficiencies from the IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report \citep[AR5, Appendix 8.A of][]{IPCC_AR5-WG1-Ch-8_2013}
whereas we use radiative efficiencies from AR6 (Section~\ref{ssec:methods-equivalent-species}).
If the CMIP6 CFC12-eq is recalculated using AR6 radiative efficiencies,
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-cmip6-recalculated", "unit": "ppt", "upper": 8}
% value-check: {"tag": "cfc12eq-diff-from-cmip6-recalculated-radiative-effect", "unit": "W/m^2", "upper": 0.0035, "lower": 0.0025}
the difference from our dataset reduces to below 8~ppt (\textasciitilde{}0.003~W/m2),
demonstrating that the underlying concentration estimates are very similar.

\subsubsection{HFC-134a-eq}
\label{sssec:results-equivalence-datasets-hfc134aeq}

% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-last-10-years-trend", "unit": "ppt/yr", "lower": 0}
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-last-10-years-second-derivative", "unit": "ppt/yr/yr", "lower": 0}
% ZNTODO: add check of lat. gradient
HFC-134a-eq has continued to rise at an accelerating rate (Figure~\ref{fig:results-hfc134aeq}).
The latitudinal gradient is notable for this equivalence species while there is almost no seasonal cycle.
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-diff-from-igcc-last-year", "unit": "ppt", "upper": 135, "lower": 115}
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-diff-from-igcc-last-year-radiative-effect", "unit": "W/m^2", "upper": 0.025, "lower": 0.015}
Our dataset is around 125~ppt higher than the IGCC dataset (\textasciitilde{}0.02~W/m2).
% Deliberately not saying like for CFC12-eq, because here the difference is due to exclusion, not inclusion, by IGCC
This is driven by two factors: a) we use radiative efficiencies from AR6 \citep{IPCC_AR6-WG1-Ch-7-SM_2021}
whereas IGCC uses radiative efficiencies from \citet{hodnebrog_gwps-efficiencies_2020}
and b) the IGCC dataset excludes gases which we include.
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-diff-from-igcc-included-gases-share", "unit": "dimensionless", "upper": 0.95, "lower": 0.85}
Of these two, the IGCC's exclusion of any gas which is not an HFC
(e.g. PFCs, SF_6, NF_3) is the dominant one,
contributing approximately 90\% of the difference.
As for CFC12-eq, the key takeaway is that
the definition and calculation methodology for this equivalent species matters
and the IGCC products are not calculated in the same way as our CMIP forcing datasets.
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-diff-from-igcc-max-radiative-effect", "unit": "W/m^2", "upper": 0.05}
Nonetheless, in approximate radiative forcing terms the difference is less than 0.05~W/m2
so the distinction may not always be a crucial one to recognise.

The dataset has changed minorly from the CMIP6 dataset.
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-diff-from-cmip6-all", "unit": "ppt", "upper": 3, "lower": 2}
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-diff-from-cmip6-all-year", "unit": "yr", "upper": 1985, "lower": 1975}
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-diff-from-cmip6-all-radiative-effect", "unit": "W/m^2", "upper": 0.0006, "lower": 0.0004}
Over the length of the dataset, the maximum deviation is 2.5~ppt in 1980 (\textasciitilde{}0.0005~W/m2).
% value-check: {"tag": "hfc134aeq-diff-from-cmip6-concentration-share", "unit": "dimensionless", "upper": 0.7, "lower": 0.3}
This is driven by both updates to the input data used to create the dataset
and the radiative efficiencies used in the calculation of the equivalent concentrations.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The output dataset shows differences from its inputs, other observation networks and the CMIP6 dataset
that are small in the context of earth system modelling (Sect.~\ref{sec:results}).

There are nonetheless a number of points to discuss and areas for potential improvement.

The first reiterates a point from Sect.~\ref{sec:output-dataset-requirements}:
this dataset is primarily produced for use in CMIP and has been evaluated by us and users within that lens.
While it may be suitable in other contexts, that is not guaranteed.
For example, for inversion studies, the raw observations themselves with appropriate uncertainty information
are likely a better source.
Similarly, the pre-1850 period has been evaluated much less carefully than the post-1850 period.
As a result, paleoclimate studies should consider whether the dataset is appropriate for their use
and whether issues like the use of Law Dome data \citep{rubino_law-dome-dataset_2019}
rather than the smoothed \citet{menking_law-dome_2025} dataset for CH_4 matter for their use case
(this is because of a configuration error, which only affects CH_4, Sect.~\ref{sssec:methods-ch4-extend}).

The second is that we are producing the forcings, and not the teams that run the observation networks.
As we understand it, to date, the observation networks and earth system modelling communities have had relatively little direct contact.
Instead, teams that produce forcings (like us) have acted as `bridges' between the two:
translating observation products into the spatiotemporally complete datasets required for use as model inputs (i.e. forcings).
While we have developed expertise in acting as this bridge,
we are not experts in either the raw observations or earth system modelling.
There is scope for investigating whether better outcomes would be achieved if the observation and earth system modelling communities worked more closely.
As a readily available example, the AGAGE community already produces a model-based reanalysis
that provides global coverage with spatial and temporal variation.
This could make our analysis redundant and the earth system modelling community could use this AGAGE product (or a variation thereof) directly.
It would also mean that decisions about pollution events could be made based on more careful considerations:
observation networks could provide insight into what these pollution events are,
modelling teams could provide insight into what the input is meant to represent
and between the two groups they could figure out which data points to include and which to exclude.
In our dataset, the inclusion of pollution events when we use NOAA's HATS flask data directly (Sect.~\ref{sssec:methods-cfc12-like-collect-observations})
is likely giving too much weight to these pollution events.

To date, no investigation has been done into the pros and cons of having observation networks become forcing providers
and the resources to do such an investigation have not been available.
Nonetheless, our suspicion is that we would get a better result if the AGAGE team produced a product that matched ESM needs directly,
rather than having us act as an intermediary.
Such an investigation would have to consider a number of questions,
for example whether modelling-based or statistical approaches to interpolation are better for the intended use cases
(given that modelling-based approaches are generally more grounded in physics, but are also more prone to producing extreme results),
how observation providers could expand their data products to provide the required temporal coverage
and which observation networks/data sources should be used given that there can be more than one provider
but, to date, only one set of forcing data has been produced,
so a choice has to be made about `preferred' providers or how to make the composite.

The use of crude scalings to avoid unphysical values is a clear area for improvement.
This is most visible for the CFC-12-like and C_4F_10-like gases,
where we have to scale down the latitudinal gradient and the seasonality to avoid negative values in the output
(Sects.~\ref{sssec:methods-cfc12-like-prepare-components} and \ref{sssec:methods-c4f10-like-prepare-components}).
The need for this points to three inconsistencies.
The first is between our pre-industrial values and the emissions used to extend the latitudinal gradient.
The global-, annual-mean is held at its pre-industrial value until the pre-industrial year and only rises slowly after it,
whereas the latitudinal gradient follows emissions, which can already be well above zero in these years.
A better approach would derive the components in a way that guarantees their consistency,
for example by constraining the latitudinal gradient and the seasonality relative to the global-mean when they are derived and extended
or by considering the consistency between the emissions estimates and concentration changes.

Including uncertainty estimates in our output would also represent another major improvement.
The value of this improvement would come less from the quantification of uncertainty itself
(we do not expect these uncertainties to be large in the context of earth system modelling)
than from the context it would provide for the dataset, which is currently lacking.
For example, the inclusion of uncertainties would help identify the leading uncertainty.
We suspect that the leading uncertainty is not related to measurement error.
Instead, it is methodological: the current method requires a number of modelling steps/assumptions
to go from the sparse observation networks we have available to a spatially and temporally complete dataset
over the entire earth's surface from year 1 to approximately present-day.
The error in this term will have specific characteristics.
For example, it will vary strongly over time:
far less modelling is required to make a spatially complete estimate in the period where we have observation networks
than when our only estimates come from one or two ice core records.

For example, IGCC \citep{forster_igcc_2026} reports uncertainties in global-mean surface concentrations
of 0.4~ppm for CO_2, 3.3~ppb for CH_4 and 0.4~ppb for N_2O.

Given that our CH_4 global-mean differs from IGCC's by around 10~ppb on average from 1983 onwards (Sect.~\ref{ssec:results-ch4}),
despite both being based on the same NOAA and AGAGE observations,
it seems that the reported uncertainty estimates exclude this methodological uncertainty.
The importance of this component will vary by gas.
It is likely particularly important for CH_4,
given that concentrations of CH_4 vary strongly in space and the underlying observation networks do not have global coverage.
In our experience, consideration of the question of methodological uncertainty is generally missing
and quantified uncertainties would help a) inform users of the origin of uncertainty,
b) encourage observation networks to engage more directly with the question of how to reduce the error
that is introduced between raw observations and an estimate of spatially and temporally complete values
and c) identify where effort should be directed to reduce uncertainty in greenhouse gas forcing datasets.

More robust methods would help reduce the methodological uncertainty.
One key example of this is our interpolation methods.
These are very simple: linear interpolation between available data points
with constant poleward extensions as needed.
Such methods clearly do not capture any more complex physics or chemistry.
Another example is our representation of the latitudinal gradient and seasonal cycles.
While our simple method produces results that largely agree with other data sources (Sect.~\ref{sec:results}),
more carefully considered methods could lead to improvements,
such as addressing our slightly too strong CH_4 seasonality (Sect.~\ref{ssec:results-ch4}).

Another option to address the interpolation uncertainty would be to use satellite observations as an input dataset.
While satellite information is only available for a limited number of gases and a limited time period,
it provides a completely different set of spatiotemporal characteristics than the ground-based network.
The inclusion of information from satellite data could therefore enhance the available input dataset
and improve the output data product.

The use of relatively crude harmonisation is also worth consideration.
Harmonisation is necessary to avoid jumps in our composite output dataset.
However, currently we use simple methods that align datasets based on a single year of data
with a declining influence of harmonisation as we move further away from the harmonisation year.
This is crude and prone to over-corrections: if the year is anomalously high or low,
that anomaly is included in every year of the harmonised dataset.

%     but by -0.05 to 2.0 ppm in individual years (1.2 ppm in 1981 vs NOAA's global-mean),

While harmonisation is difficult, especially automated harmonisation,
the use of a multi-year average or offsets that follow the interhemispheric gradient
could guard against some of these potential problems.

Another issue, albeit a relatively minor one,
is that we do not convert between the calibration scales of the different observation networks,
nor have we explored the differences between them in depth.
For the gases with the largest radiative forcing, the effect is negligible:
we only use data from NOAA for CO_2,
the difference between the AGAGE and NOAA scales for CH_4 is less than 0.1\%
(\citealp{meinshausen_historical-ghgs_2017} converted AGAGE CH_4 to the NOAA scale by multiplying by 1.0003)
and the AGAGE and NOAA scales for N_2O are compatible without conversion
\citep{meinshausen_historical-ghgs_2017}.
For halocarbons, the differences between calibration scales are small
and these gases have smaller radiative forcings.
Nonetheless, a more careful treatment of calibration scales,
ideally developed together with the observation networks,
would be an improvement.

To complete consideration of issues, we list other minor issues that have not been mentioned yet.
The first is that, like \citet{meinshausen_historical-ghgs_2017}, we do not provide any information on the vertical dimension.
In addition, like \citet{meinshausen_historical-ghgs_2017}, we do not provide any information on the longitudinal dimension
despite the possibility for variations in this dimension.
Finally, our files use CF standard names \citep{eaton_cf-conventions_2026}
of the form \texttt{mole\_fraction\_of\_X\_in\_air}
rather than the more explicit \texttt{mole\_fraction\_of\_X\_in\_dry\_air}.

In future, we would like to produce annual updates of this forcing dataset.
Such annual updates would ameliorate the time pressure to deliver for a specific CMIP cycle:
there would always be the most recent annual update available.
This delivery could also be coupled to more regular and automated validation of the datasets,
such as via a Rapid Evaluation Framework for forcings (REF for forcings)
that would apply the ideas from the REF \citep{climate-ref_docs_2026,hassler_ref_2026,hoffman_ref_2026} to forcings datasets.
If we were to produce annual updates, this may encourage inclusion of more data sources as inputs or validation sources.
It would also prompt consideration
of how best to handle gases which do not have regularly updated observations, such as C_8F_18,
and which gases exactly should be included in our dataset
given that there is a balance to be struck between coverage, effort,
impact on output data (given that many gases have small, or even negligible radiative forcings)
and availability of recent observations.

The produced GHG concentration forcings are available and consistent with the underlying emissions.
The fact that the experiment design requires delivery of a single estimate for each experiment (Sect.~\ref{sec:output-dataset-requirements})
leads to one key consequence: emissions-driven simulations from ESMs will almost certainly not exactly reproduce the provided GHG concentrations.
This cannot be avoided.
We cannot use an ESM to produce the GHG forcings in the first place because of their computational expense (Sect.~\ref{ssec:methods-motivation}).
Even if we did pick a single ESM to use to produce the GHG forcings,
ESMs will likely not all produce the same output in their emissions-driven simulations
so the picked forcing dataset would still disagree with all other ESM outputs.
This disagreement between the provided GHG concentration forcings
and output from ESMs is a known consequence of the experiment design.

There are a number of discussion points and improvements worth returning to.
The first is the use of harmonisation.
Harmonisation is difficult in all contexts in which it is used [TODO cite e.g. Kikstra, Gidden].
The need for it is an indication that there is a mismatch between the timeseries being harmonised to, usually a historical timeseries,
the method/timeseries being harmonised, usually a projection timeseries based on modelling of some sort.
Harmonisation then has to resolve this mismatch.
As illustrated in this paper, there is no perfect way to do this.
Using no harmonisation leads to a spurious jump in the output at the time point when we transition from the historical to projection timeseries.
Never returning to the projection timeseries means that the difference at the harmonisation point
affects every projected time point too, which does not always make physical sense (e.g. for short-lived emissions in this paper).
Between these two extremes, a tradeoff is being made between having a faster transition to the projected timeseries (and therefore risking introducing unphysical features)
and persisting the difference from historical over all time (which can also introduce unphysical features).

The fact that the harmonisation effect also depends on the harmonisation time,
therefore the experiment in this case (the harmonisation effect for VL differs notably from that for VL-CF, [TODO ref section]),
is another clear undesirable side effect.
The answer to this is to minimise the difference between the historical timeseries and the projection method,
therefore reducing the possible impact of harmonisation.
For our particular use case, the area for future improvement is clear:
improve our calibration processes so that we can produce a calibration of MAGICC
which agrees closely with the historical timeseries within the short forcing preparation timelines available.
This would have allowed us to run MAGICC with CMIP7 concentrations for the historical period,
rather than the CMIP6 concentrations we used because of the calibration's tight coupling with the CMIP6 historical timeseries.
Ideally, this calibration process would be sufficiently sophisticated that it would maintain agreement with key assessments,
like AR6, while taking into account the updated historical timeseries.
This is certainly challenging, but it is also doable.

Being able to re-calibrate MAGICC on shorter timescales would solve another identified issue,
the use of a one-box model for projections of some gases rather than MAGICC.
Given the small radiative forcing of these gases (compared to total radiative forcing),
this was an acceptable choice given the delivery timelines we were required to meet.
Nonetheless, an updated calibration of MAGICC, including updating lifetimes to match the latest literature estimates,
would resolve this issue too.

The third key note is that the datasets produced here are not exhaustive.
Due to the computational expense of ESMs, only a limited number of experiments can be run.
We only produce forcings for this limited set,
so the range of forcings included here should not be confused with the `full range' of possible future GHG concentrations:
that full range is wider than shown here, particularly in the relationship across GHGs
(the GHG concentration forcings produced here tend to be correlated across scenarios
i.e. higher CO_2 concentrations implies higher CH_4 concentrations).

The fourth point to consider is the use of a single source for projections of ozone-depleting substances.
In some ways, this is at odds with the overall scenario narrative that underpins ScenarioMIP scenarios.
While it is true that the Montreal Agreement should constrain the evolution of concentrations of these species,
concentrations of other species explore a range of futures,
both ones compliant with international agreements and ones that are not.
Not applying the same logic to the ozone-depleting substances results in a small inconsistency
that may be worth further consideration.

The fifth issue is inconsistencies between experiments.

As a result of the tight delivery timelines,
differences between VL-CF and the rest of the experiments emerged that were not intended.
These are all related to the fact that VL-CF diverges from history in 2016.
The issue relates to CO_2's seasonality change and the latitudinal gradient used for CO_2, CH_4 and N_2O.
In short, the regression with historical drivers was re-calculated over the period up until 2015 only,
rather than over the full historical period as was done for the ScenarioMIP experiments.
Therefore the sensitivity of the seasonality for CO_2 and latitudinal gradients for CO_2, CH_4 and N_2O
to their drivers is different in VL-CF than in the ScenarioMIP scenarios, which doesn't make physical sense.

This should be rectified in future work and we will endeavour to release an updated version of VL-CF without this issue in case any ESM team wishes to quantify the impact of this difference.
Based on the work in \citet{meinshausen_ssp-ghgs_2020},
which showed that the sensitivity of projections to latitudinal gradients and seasonality was small,
we expect the impact to be small and therefore have not recommended that any simulations that use the existing VL-CF dataset be re-run.

The sixth issue is inconsistencies between the methods used for the historical dataset and for the projections.
Specifically, NPP is used to drive seasonality change in the future
but a composite of global-, annual-mean concentrations and temperature is used to drive the same changes in the historical dataset \citep{nicholls_historical-ghgs_2026}.
In addition, the latitudinal gradient is based on regressions that use different emissions sets.
For the gases for which we re-derive the regressions (CO_2, CH_4 and N_2O),
we use the CMIP7 historical emissions, rather than the emissions used during the production of the historical dataset.
For the other gases, we use the historical dataset's regressions,
which are against the SSP2-4.5 emissions,
but then apply them with the CMIP7 emissions.

These introduce further inconsistencies in seasonality and latitudinal gradients
that should be reconsidered in future work.

The seventh issue is the need to use a method which can produce datasets that span from year 1 to 2500 or beyond.
This limits the level of sophistication which can be used as there are only a few ways to get information over such a long timespan.
While there are no obvious solutions here, a number of elements could be considered more carefully and perhaps updated.
The first is the driver for changes in the latitudinal gradient.
While a linear regression against emissions works well for some species,
for others (most notably CH_4) it is a much less good fit.
The second, like also affected the historical dataset \citep{nicholls_historical-ghgs_2026}, is the need to scale latitudinal gradients and seasonality down
in order to avoid negative values appearing in the output datasets.
More careful consideration of the method as a whole
and how to preserve internal consistency
may lead to better results or identification of improved methods
that nonetheless meet the constraints set by the output dataset requirements.

The last issue is the difference between inversions of the CMIP7 concentrations and the CMIP7 extended emissions,
and the fact that we run MAGICC in emissions-driven mode for gases where the CMIP7 concentrations are defined by an external source.
Both of these lead to internal inconsistencies, albeit minor ones in the wider picture.
Nonetheless, they point to the need for careful coordination between the multiple teams involved in producing forcings.
While CMIP7 has achieved a much greater coherence than was achieved in CMIP6,
there is still room for improvement, which we hope that future work can address.
